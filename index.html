Common Question: Why is "héng zhé" NOT called "héng shù"? Why is "shù zhé" NOT called "shù héng"?
A single Chinese stroke is defined as writing in ONE continuous motion without lifting the pen from the paper. Naming it "héng shù" would sound like TWO separate strokes (1st stroke: héng, 2nd stroke: shù). Because the direction turns in the middle of a single stroke, that turning motion is named zhé (Turn). Therefore, "héng" + turn = héng zhé, and "shù" + turn = shù zhé!

Common Question: Why is "xié gōu" NOT called "nà gōu"?
A standard nà stroke gradually thickens and ends with a smooth, tapered sweep toward the bottom-right — its shape cannot naturally end with a sharp upward hook flick. xié gōu has a slanted vertical stem with a sharp, upward flick pointing straight toward 12 o'clock, so it is classified as a slanted vertical hook (xié gōu), never "nà gōu"!

10. Lesson Wrap-Up & Key Takeaways

Core Points Summary:

  • "What You Read Is What You Write": Decode complex stroke names by treating basic stroke names as action steps (zhé = turn, gōu = hook).
  • Continuous Writing: All strokes with turns (zhé) and hooks (gōu) must be written in one continuous movement without lifting your pen.
  • Square Balance: Fill the imaginary square grid evenly, ensuring proper proportions whether the character has 2 or 10 strokes.
  • Stroke Order Master Rules: Remember héng before shù, piě before nà, and fill inside before sealing enclosed frames!
